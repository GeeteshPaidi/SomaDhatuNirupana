\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Sensitivity of retrieved weight ratios to the solar spectrum}\label{sec:solar_sens}
Element/Si ratios are sometimes expected to be insensitive to the incident spectrum. Our forward model indicates that the sensitivity is appreciable. Each K line is excited by the part of the solar spectrum above its own edge (Mg \SI{1.31}{keV}, Si \SI{1.84}{keV}, Ca \SI{4.04}{keV}), and at coronal temperatures that part is dominated by a few He-like line complexes whose relative strengths change steeply with temperature.

In our synthetic tests, a spectrum 30\% hotter than assumed biases Mg/Si by \SynHotMg\% and changes Ca/Si by an order of magnitude. Refitting real data with the GOES temperature scaled by 0.8 and 1.2 changes the median Mg/Si by \TsensMgLo\% and \TsensMgHi\%, and Al/Si by \TsensAlLo\% and \TsensAlHi\%.

Mg/Si is about three times as sensitive as Al/Si because of where the K edges lie relative to the solar lines. Photons above the Si K edge (\SI{1.84}{keV}) excite all three elements, so changes in that part of the spectrum largely cancel in both ratios. The ratios therefore respond mainly to photons that excite the numerator element but not Si. For Al this band is narrow (\SIrange{1.56}{1.84}{keV}) and contains only weak lines (e.g.\ Al\,\textsc{xii}). For Mg it is wider (\SIrange{1.30}{1.84}{keV}) and contains the strong Mg\,\textsc{xi} and Mg\,\textsc{xii} lines, whose emission relative to the Si\,\textsc{xiii} and S\,\textsc{xv} complexes changes with temperature. Weighting the CHIANTI spectrum by an $E^{-2.7}$ photoabsorption cross-section above each edge, the ratio of Mg to Si excitation falls by a factor of 3.9 between 3 and \SI{8}{MK}, whereas that of Al to Si falls by a factor of 1.6.

\subsection{GOES as a proxy for the solar spectrum}\label{sec:goes_proxy}
GOES provides an estimate of the solar state for every CLASS exposure from public data, with known limitations. The two-channel temperature is a single isothermal value, weighted towards the hottest plasma, and below the B1 level it is unreliable; in our data it was pinned to the edge of the response table or diverged there. The GOES-16 and GOES-18 temperatures agree to 0.5\% in their overlap.

In the synthetic tests at \SI{5}{MK}, which are generated and fitted with the same isothermal spectrum, Ca/Si is poorly constrained because such a spectrum barely excites Ca (\cref{sec:synthetic}). In the observations, a further error can arise because the real corona is multi-thermal and the isothermal approximation underpredicts the flux above \SI{4}{keV} at the temperatures typical of our data; this affects Ca, Ti and Fe most. The residual orbit-to-orbit striping in the maps is consistent with solar-spectrum errors of this kind, although we have not isolated its cause.

\subsection{Comparison with XSM solar spectra}\label{sec:xsm}
To test the GOES approximation directly, we used spectra of the Chandrayaan-2 Solar X-ray Monitor \citep{Mithun2020} for three months chosen in advance (December 2024, June 2025 and December 2025). We generated 1-minute Level-2 spectra from the public Level-1 data with the instrument team's software (XSMDAS v1.5, calibration database of 2025 November 11), using its standard good-time filter and area scaling. XSM sees the Sun only when the Sun is within its field of view. Of the 92 days of these months, we obtained XSM Level-1 files for 91, and the Sun was in the field of view on 62 of them, for part of each orbit. Its 1-minute spectra overlap \XsmNClass{} mapping-set CLASS exposures (\XsmNMin{} XSM minutes). The months, the procedure and the statistics reported below were chosen before the test was run; no numerical acceptance threshold was set. We fitted each XSM minute in \SIrange{1.5}{10}{keV} with the same CHIANTI table, using the response matrix of the XSM calibration database, first with a single temperature and then with two temperatures. We then refitted the overlapping CLASS spectra with the XSM-derived spectrum in place of the GOES one, keeping everything else unchanged. Refitting with the GOES temperature reproduces the mapped values exactly. Of the overlapping exposures, \XsmOneN{} have good fits (quality 0) with both the GOES and the isothermal XSM spectrum, \XsmTwoN{} with both the GOES and the two-temperature spectrum, and \XsmNBoth{} with all three. Each median below uses the corresponding subset; \cref{tab:xsmregimes} uses the common subset.

The XSM isothermal temperatures are higher than the GOES values by a median factor of \XsmTRatio{} (16th--84th percentile \XsmTRatioLo--\XsmTRatioHi). With the XSM isothermal spectrum, the median Al/Si changes by \XsmOneAl\% (16th--84th percentile \XsmOneAlLo{} to \XsmOneAlHi\%) and the median Mg/Si by \XsmOneMg\% (\XsmOneMgLo{} to \XsmOneMgHi\%; \XsmOneN{} spectra, \cref{fig:xsm}). With the two-temperature XSM spectrum, the changes are \XsmTwoAl\% for Al/Si and \XsmTwoMg\% for Mg/Si (\XsmTwoN{} spectra). The per-month medians are listed in \cref{tab:xsm}.

The two descriptions have a physical interpretation. XSM spectra of the active Sun are commonly described by a cooler component from the non-flaring active-region and quiet corona and, during flares, a hotter component from the flaring loops \citep{Mondal2021,Ng2024}. An isothermal fit returns a single compromise temperature, weighted towards whichever component dominates the 1.5--\SI{10}{keV} counts. For lunar fluorescence, what matters is the summed photon spectrum of both components above the K edges. The two-temperature model fits the measured \SIrange{1.5}{10}{keV} band better, but that does not establish that its extrapolation to the Mg-exciting flux below the fitted band is closer to the truth, so we treat the difference between the two descriptions as a sensitivity range. The non-flaring solar emission is part of this excitation spectrum on the dayside; it is distinct from the detector background, which we measure in the lunar umbra (\cref{sec:background}).

To see whether the comparison depends on solar activity, we grouped the exposures by relative flare enhancement $F$, the GOES XRS-B flux divided by its 12-hour running 10th percentile. Absolute GOES classes are not useful for this purpose in these months, because the background flux near solar maximum is itself at C level: all of the compared exposures have an XRS-B flux of at least B5, and 96\% of them at least C1. \Cref{tab:xsmregimes} lists the results for three groups: near the local baseline ($F<1.5$), moderate enhancement, mostly weak flares or flare decay ($1.5\le F<3$), and strong enhancement, i.e.\ flares ($F\geq3$). The XSM/GOES temperature ratio increases slightly with activity (\XsmRegLoTRatio{} to \XsmRegHiTRatio), but the median changes in both ratios are of similar magnitude in all three groups: Al/Si changes by \XsmRegLoOneAl{} to \XsmRegMidOneAl\% with the isothermal and by \XsmRegLoTwoAl{} to \XsmRegHiTwoAl\% with the two-temperature spectrum, and Mg/Si by \XsmRegHiOneMg{} to \XsmRegMidOneMg\% and \XsmRegLoTwoMg{} to \XsmRegHiTwoMg\%, respectively. This compares model-dependent shifts between groups in the quality-selected overlap sample; it does not measure the accuracy of GOES within each group. The lowest-enhancement group represents intervals near the local baseline, which in these months is itself at C level; genuinely quiet-Sun and separately identified pre-flare intervals remain outside the scope of this test. We use a uniform GOES isothermal model for mapping and use the paired XSM isothermal and two-temperature refits to quantify the sensitivity to the assumed excitation spectrum.

Two conclusions follow. First, median Al/Si shifts are a few per cent under either description of the measured solar spectrum, although individual spectra scatter more widely (\cref{tab:xsmregimes}); this supports Al/Si as the more robust product. Second, Mg/Si depends noticeably on the assumed thermal structure of the corona: the isothermal and two-temperature descriptions of the same XSM spectra shift it in opposite directions. Both shifts lie within the range obtained by scaling the GOES temperature by 0.8 and 1.2 (\TsensMgLo\% to \TsensMgHi\%; \cref{sec:limitations}), but they show that the solar excitation is a major source of Mg/Si uncertainty. Neither description fits the XSM spectra perfectly (median reduced $\chi^2$ of \XsmChiOne{} and \XsmChiTwo{}), plausibly because of variable coronal abundances, so this comparison constrains, rather than removes, the solar-spectrum systematic. A full XSM-based treatment of all months, ideally with free abundances, is a natural extension, and our model accepts any tabulated $S(E)$. A pixel-by-pixel comparison with the XSM-based abundance maps of \citet{Narendranath2024} would be the most direct external test of our maps; we could not locate a public release of those maps, so we compare with GRS and returned samples instead.

\subsection{The instrumental aluminium component}
The Al K$\alpha$ excess in sunlit nightside spectra has a sharp onset near \texttt{SOLARANG} $\approx93^\circ$ and disappears at the shadow boundary (\cref{fig:background}). The most likely explanation is solar X-rays reaching aluminium in the instrument (filters, frames, housing; \citealp{Pillai2021}) when the Sun is below the plane of the instrument's Moon-facing side and can illuminate it directly (\cref{sec:background}). On the dayside the Sun is behind the instrument relative to its viewing direction, that side is shadowed, and we expect the component to be negligible. It is significant only in sunlit nightside spectra. It matters for dayside Al/Si because nightside spectra are used to build the background model, as follows.

Its treatment affects Al/Si appreciably. Subtracting a mean nightside spectrum can remove lunar Al signal, because the instrumental Al component varies among sunlit nightside exposures. Fitting its amplitude freely, as in our alternative model, lets it compete with the lunar Al line: in a fixed farside region the monthly median Al/Si anticorrelates with the fraction of spectra with a non-zero fitted amplitude (\cref{sec:alt}). With the settings of the first analysis stage, freeing the amplitude lowered the GRS correlation of Al/Si from \GrsAlRvThree{} to \GrsAlRvTwo, while that of Mg/Si was unchanged (\GrsMgRvThree{} and \GrsMgRvTwo). Other XRF instruments with aluminium structures or filters could show a similar effect, which the same nightside test would reveal.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Approximate solar spectrum.} The solar spectrum is isothermal at the GOES temperature. In synthetic tests, temperature errors of $+30$\% and $-20$\% bias Mg/Si by \SynHotMg\% and \SynColdMg\%, and Al/Si by \SynHotAl\% and \SynColdAl\%. Scaling the GOES temperature of real data by 0.8 and 1.2 changes Mg/Si by \TsensMgLo\% and \TsensMgHi\%, and Al/Si by \TsensAlLo\% and \TsensAlHi\% (\cref{sec:solar_sens}). The XSM comparison for three months (\cref{sec:xsm}) gives changes within these ranges. Ca/Si, Ti/Si and Fe/Si are not retrieved with this approach.
\item \emph{Ratios, not absolute abundances.} The products are elemental weight ratios to Si derived through the forward model; they are neither line-flux ratios nor absolute concentrations. No absolute flux calibration is used: the amplitude $A$ absorbs the solar flux, area and geometry. Errors in the relative efficiency of the Mg, Al and Si lines would rescale the ratios (item 3).
\item \emph{Detector efficiency.} The energy dependence of the distributed ARF is applied, and all SCDs are assumed to have the same response, so that summing fewer than 16 devices only rescales the amplitude. The ARF is a pre-launch product; any error in the relative efficiency at the Mg, Al and Si lines, for example from in-flight changes of the devices, would rescale the ratios. Both ratios are higher than the returned soils (mean site bias \SiteMgTwoBias{} for Mg/Si and \SiteAlTwoBias{} for Al/Si, with median Al/Si \MedAl), consistent with such a scale error. A scale error would leave the pattern and the correlations of the maps unchanged.
\item \emph{Physical effects not modelled.} Particle-size and roughness effects, phase-angle dependence beyond the plane-parallel geometry, and the angular dependence of scattering are not modelled.
\item \emph{Spatial resolution.} Footprints are $25\times37$\,km. The maps are 2\textdegree{} and should not be interpreted at finer scales.
\item \emph{Small validation sample.} Nine landing sites, sampled at the scale of a footprint, give a test with little statistical power. The GRS comparison is limited by the GRS resolution and by its own model dependence for Mg and Al.
\end{enumerate}
